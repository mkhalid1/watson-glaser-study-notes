# The Watson-Glaser test explained

The Watson-Glaser Critical Thinking Appraisal is a timed test of how well you reason from written information. You read short passages and judge statements about them: whether an inference is true, whether an assumption is being made, whether a conclusion follows, and whether an argument is strong. It is published by Pearson TalentLens, which lists law among the industries it serves, and UK law firms such as Clifford Chance use it when recruiting for training contracts and vacation schemes.

## Quick facts

| | What the publisher says |
|---|---|
| Publisher | Pearson TalentLens |
| Current edition | Watson-Glaser III, which draws each candidate's questions from a large item bank |
| Questions | 40 |
| Time | 30 to 40 minutes, timed |
| What it measures | Critical thinking: analysing arguments, evaluating information and drawing logical conclusions from written material |
| Subtests | Drawing inferences, Recognising assumptions, Deducing, Interpreting, Evaluating arguments |
| Score areas | Recognise Assumptions, Evaluate Arguments, Draw Conclusions (the RED model) |
| Report | An overall percentile plus a percentile for each of the three areas |
| Languages | UK English, US English, French, French Canadian, Dutch, Spanish, Latin American Spanish |
| Main uses | Recruitment, development, assessment and mobility of graduates, executives, managers, supervisors and professionals. Pearson also notes use by university selection teams. |

Sources: [Pearson TalentLens product page](https://www.talentlens.com/watson-glaser.html) and [Pearson's 2020 Watson-Glaser efficacy report](https://www.talentlens.com/content/dam/school/global/Global-Talentlens/uk/CriticalThinking/CTHub/Pearson_Efficacy_Watson-Glaser_2020.pdf). Checked on 24 November 2026.

## The five subtests and the three scores

You answer five kinds of question, but your report groups them into three areas. Pearson calls this the RED model.

| Subtest | What you decide | Reported under |
|---|---|---|
| [Inference](sections/inference.md) | How likely a statement is to be true, on a five-point scale from True to False | Draw Conclusions |
| [Recognising assumptions](sections/recognising-assumptions.md) | Whether the speaker is taking something for granted | Recognise Assumptions |
| [Deduction](sections/deduction.md) | Whether a conclusion must follow from the premises | Draw Conclusions |
| [Interpretation](sections/interpretation.md) | Whether a conclusion follows beyond reasonable doubt from the evidence | Draw Conclusions |
| [Evaluation of arguments](sections/evaluation-of-arguments.md) | Whether an argument is strong or weak for the question asked | Evaluate Arguments |

Pearson's efficacy report states the split of the 40 items by area: **12 Recognise Assumptions, 12 Evaluate Arguments and 16 Draw Conclusions**. Draw Conclusions covers three subtests (inference, deduction and interpretation), so those three share 16 items between them.

What this means for your preparation: assumptions and arguments are two subtests carrying 24 of the 40 items, 12 each. They are also the two that people tend to practise least.

## How the test is built

- **Every candidate gets a different set of questions.** Watson-Glaser III draws items from a bank. Pearson says each item is coded by difficulty and each passage by content theme, so every test has a similar mix of difficulty and topics. Small differences in difficulty between versions are allowed for in the scoring.
- **Some passages are neutral and some are charged.** Pearson's efficacy report describes two kinds of scenario: neutral ones (weather, scientific facts, ordinary business situations) and controversial ones (political, economic and social issues). The charged passages are there on purpose. They test whether you can reason about a topic you have strong views on without letting those views decide your answer.
- **It is often taken unsupervised online.** Pearson says the test suits unproctored online screening as well as supervised settings, with instant results.

## Why law firms use it

Critical thinking is close to the daily work of a junior lawyer: reading a document, spotting what is assumed but not said, and separating what the facts prove from what they merely suggest. Pearson's efficacy report also cites studies that compared Watson-Glaser scores with final grades on the Bar Professional Training Course, the vocational course for aspiring barristers in England and Wales, and on a business degree, and found positive correlations.

Firms publish their own process. One example, from [Clifford Chance's London hiring page](https://jobs.cliffordchance.com/how-we-hire-london): after the online application, candidates complete the firm's Immersive Online Assessment and then the Watson Glaser test, with four days (including weekends) to complete the Watson Glaser. The deadlines arrive by email once the application is submitted. Clifford Chance's [FAQ](https://jobs.cliffordchance.com/faq-london) adds that a passed score is saved for two recruitment cycles. For any other firm, read that firm's own application page: processes change every cycle. The [law applicants guide](law-applicants.md) covers this in more depth.

## Versions you may hear about

- **Watson-Glaser II** introduced the RED model in 2010 and used fixed forms (everyone answered the same questions).
- **Watson-Glaser III** is the current edition and uses the item bank.
- The test was first published in 1964 by Goodwin Watson and Edward M. Glaser.

Older practice booklets may follow a different layout from the online test you will sit. The question rules, which are what you need to learn, are the same.

## The one rule that runs through the whole test

Treat the passage as true and work only from it. The test is not asking what you know about the topic, or what you believe. A conclusion can be sensible, popular, even correct in the real world, and still be the wrong answer because the passage does not support it.
