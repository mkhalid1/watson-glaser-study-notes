# Recognising assumptions

**In one line:** you read a statement and decide whether the person making it is taking a given idea for granted.

This subtest has its own score area, **Recognise Assumptions**, and Pearson's efficacy report puts 12 of the 40 items in it. That makes it one of the two biggest single subtests on the test.

## What it measures

Pearson describes this subtest as identifying unstated assumptions or presuppositions underlying given statements. An assumption is something the speaker needs to be true for their statement to make sense, but never says.

In legal work this is the skill of reading a clause, a client email or an opponent's letter and asking: what does this quietly rely on? If the hidden premise is wrong, the whole point falls over.

## How the questions look

A short statement, often a piece of advice, a plan or a prediction, with a reason attached. Several proposed assumptions follow. For each one you answer:

| Answer | Choose it when |
|---|---|
| **Assumption made** | The speaker must be taking this for granted, whether or not it is justified. |
| **Assumption not made** | The statement can make full sense without the speaker believing this. |

Judge each proposed assumption on its own. A statement can rest on several assumptions at once, or on none of the ones offered.

## A method that works

1. **Find the claim and the reason.** Most statements have the shape "do X, because Y" or "X will happen, because Y". Split them.
2. **Ask what links the reason to the claim.** The assumption usually lives in the gap. "We should book now, because prices rise" relies on the listener caring about the price.
3. **Run the denial test.** Imagine the proposed assumption is false. Does the speaker's statement now look silly or pointless? If yes, the assumption is made. If the statement still makes sense, it is not made.
4. **Check it is necessary, not just likely.** "Made" means the speaker *must* be relying on it. Something the speaker probably also believes is not enough.
5. **Ignore whether the assumption is true.** You are judging what the speaker takes for granted, not whether they are right.

## Traps that cost points

- **Choosing "made" because the assumption sounds reasonable.** Many offered assumptions are sensible things the speaker might believe. Only choose "made" if the statement depends on it.
- **Confusing a prediction with a goal.** "The extension will bring in more applications" predicts an effect. It does not assume the firm *wants* more applications.
- **Over-reading strong words.** If the statement says "should", it assumes the action is possible and worth doing. It does not assume it is the *only* or *best* option, unless it says so.
- **Under-reading the obvious.** Some assumptions are so basic they feel too easy, such as "the listener plans to travel" when told to book tickets. Those are usually "made".
- **Bringing in the topic you know.** Your knowledge of how training contracts or train fares work is irrelevant. Only the statement counts.
- **Assuming extremes.** A speaker who says something will reduce time spent does not assume it will remove the task entirely.

## Timing

Assumption items are short to read, so you can afford to be careful. As guidance: aim for about 30 seconds per proposed assumption. Use the denial test every time: it takes five seconds and catches most errors.

## Practice items

These 5 items are original, written for these notes. They are not taken from the Watson-Glaser or any publisher's materials. For each statement, decide whether each proposed assumption is made or not made.

### Statement 1

> "The firm has extended its vacation scheme deadline by a week, so it will receive more applications."

**Item 1.** Some people who might apply had not yet applied by the original deadline.

**Item 2.** The applications received in the extra week will be stronger than the earlier ones.

**Item 3.** The firm wants to receive more applications.

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 1 to 3</summary>

**Item 1: Made.** If everyone who would ever apply had already done so, an extra week would bring no new applications. The prediction depends on there being people still to apply.

**Item 2: Not made.** The statement is about the *number* of applications, not their quality.

**Item 3: Not made.** This is the classic trap. The speaker *predicts* more applications. They may well think the firm wants that, but the prediction makes full sense without it. The extension could have been granted for a different reason, such as a technical fault on the application site.

</details>

### Statement 2

> "Because the new contract-review software flags missing clauses automatically, our junior lawyers will spend less time on first-pass reviews."

**Item 4.** Junior lawyers currently spend some of their first-pass review time checking for missing clauses.

**Item 5.** The software is more accurate than the junior lawyers.

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 4 and 5</summary>

**Item 4: Made.** Time can only be saved on a task the lawyers currently do. If they never checked for missing clauses, automating that check would save nothing.

**Item 5: Not made.** Saving time does not require the software to be *more* accurate. It only needs to be good enough to rely on for that step.

</details>

## Score yourself

| Item | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Answer | Made | Not | Not | Made | Not |

- **5:** strong. Keep the denial test as a habit under time pressure.
- **4:** check whether your misses were "made" answers on reasonable-sounding ideas. That is the most common pattern.
- **3 or fewer:** slow down and write the claim and the reason as two separate lines before judging each assumption.
