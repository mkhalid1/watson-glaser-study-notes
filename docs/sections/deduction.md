# Deduction

**In one line:** you take a set of premises as true without exception and decide whether a conclusion must follow from them.

Deduction is scored under **Draw Conclusions**. It has the strictest standard on the test: "probably" is never enough. The conclusion follows only if it would be impossible for the premises to be true and the conclusion false.

## What it measures

Pearson describes this subtest as determining whether conclusions follow logically from given information. It is formal logic in plain English: "all", "no", "some", "if... then" and "only".

The legal parallel is reading a statutory definition or a contract condition and working out exactly what it does and does not cover. A clause that applies to "all employees in the sales team" tells you nothing about a contractor in the sales team.

## How the questions look

Two or more short premises, then several proposed conclusions. For each conclusion you answer:

| Answer | Choose it when |
|---|---|
| **Conclusion follows** | The conclusion must be true whenever the premises are true. |
| **Conclusion does not follow** | The premises could all be true while the conclusion is false, even if the conclusion is true in real life. |

One official rule catches many people: **"some" means at least one, and possibly all.** "Some partners speak French" does not tell you that some partners do not.

## A method that works

1. **Rewrite each premise in a fixed form.** "All A are B", "No A are B", "Some A are B", "If P then Q". Name the groups precisely: "trainees in the disputes team", not "trainees".
2. **Draw it if it helps.** Two overlapping circles take five seconds and stop most errors with "all" and "some".
3. **Try to break the conclusion.** Look for one situation where every premise is true but the conclusion is false. If you can find one, the conclusion does not follow.
4. **If you cannot break it, check you used only the premises.** If your reasoning needed a fact from outside, it does not follow.
5. **Ignore real-world truth.** A conclusion that is obviously true in life can still fail. A conclusion that is absurd in life can still follow.

## Common forms to know

| Premises | Valid conclusion | Common invalid conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| All A are B. | Anything that is A is B. | All B are A. |
| All A are B. X is not B. | X is not A. | (none) |
| All A are B. X is not A. | Nothing follows about whether X is B. | X is not B. |
| If P then Q. Not Q. | Not P. | (none) |
| If P then Q. Q. | Nothing follows about P. | P. |
| Some A are B. All B are C. | Some A are C. | Some A are not C. |
| No A are B. | No B are A. | Everything that is not A is B. |

## Traps that cost points

- **Reversing "all".** "All trainees attend" does not mean "everyone who attends is a trainee."
- **Reading "some" as "some but not all".** In this test "some" can include all.
- **Affirming the consequent.** "If the budget is approved, work starts. Work started" does not prove the budget was approved.
- **Denying the antecedent.** "If the budget is approved, work starts. It was not approved" does not prove work will not start.
- **Letting real-world knowledge in.** If the premises say the firm's Aberdeen office is not in Scotland, accept it for the item.
- **Missing boundary cases.** "Not signed after 1 March" includes "signed on 1 March" and "not signed at all".
- **Merging groups.** Premises about "trainees in the disputes team" say nothing about other trainees.

## Timing

Deduction items are short but need care. As guidance: about 30 to 40 seconds per conclusion. Drawing a quick diagram the first time you see a premise set pays for itself across all its conclusions.

## Practice items

These 5 items are original, written for these notes. They are not taken from the Watson-Glaser or any publisher's materials. Treat every premise as true without exception.

### Set 1

> All trainees in the disputes team attend the Monday briefing. Some trainees in the disputes team are on client secondment.

**Item 1.** Some trainees on client secondment attend the Monday briefing.

**Item 2.** Everyone who attends the Monday briefing is a trainee in the disputes team.

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 1 and 2</summary>

**Item 1: Follows.** The disputes trainees who are on secondment are still disputes trainees, and all disputes trainees attend. So at least those secondees attend.

**Item 2: Does not follow.** This reverses the first premise. Partners, associates or trainees from other teams could also attend.

</details>

### Set 2

> If the client approves the budget by Friday, the team will start work on Monday. The team will not start work on Monday.

**Item 3.** The client will not approve the budget by Friday.

**Item 4.** The client has rejected the budget.

**Item 5.** The team will start work on Tuesday.

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 3 to 5</summary>

**Item 3: Follows.** Approval by Friday guarantees a Monday start. There will be no Monday start, so there cannot be approval by Friday.

**Item 4: Does not follow.** All the premises tell you is that approval will not come by Friday. The client could still be reviewing the budget, or approve it next week. Not approving in time is not the same as rejecting.

**Item 5: Does not follow.** Nothing in the premises mentions Tuesday or any other start date.

</details>

## Score yourself

| Item | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Answer | F | DNF | F | DNF | DNF |

F = follows, DNF = does not follow.

- **5:** your logic is solid. Watch for speed in the real test.
- **4:** check the "common forms" table against each miss. Most errors are a reversed "all" or a misread "some".
- **3 or fewer:** draw every premise set as circles for a week of practice. It builds the instinct quickly.
