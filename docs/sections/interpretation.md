# Interpretation

**In one line:** you read a short piece of evidence, often with figures, and decide whether a proposed conclusion follows beyond reasonable doubt.

Interpretation is scored under **Draw Conclusions**. Its standard sits between inference and deduction: stricter than "probably", looser than "must".

## What it measures

Pearson describes this subtest as weighing the evidence and deciding whether generalisations or conclusions based on the data are warranted. The skill is knowing how far a set of facts can carry you, and spotting where a conclusion stretches past them.

That is the daily risk in legal and commercial work: a report shows a correlation, and someone writes it up as a cause. A sample of 90 people becomes "most of our staff". This subtest checks you would catch that.

## How the questions look

A paragraph of evidence: a study, survey, set of results or short account. Several proposed conclusions follow. For each one:

| Answer | Choose it when |
|---|---|
| **Conclusion follows** | The conclusion follows beyond reasonable doubt from the paragraph, even if not with absolute logical certainty. |
| **Conclusion does not follow** | The paragraph does not support it to that standard. |

Assume everything in the paragraph is true. Judge each conclusion on its own.

## How it differs from deduction and inference

| Subtest | Standard | Options |
|---|---|---|
| Deduction | Must be true if the premises are true | Two |
| Interpretation | Follows beyond reasonable doubt | Two |
| Inference | Rated on a five-point likelihood scale | Five |

So a conclusion that is a direct reading or simple calculation from the data follows. A conclusion that adds a cause, a motive, a prediction or a wider group usually does not.

## A method that works

1. **Find exactly what was measured, of whom, and when.** Loans, not users. Applicants, not students. Respondents, not all staff.
2. **Compare the conclusion's scope with the data's scope.** Is it about the same group, the same measure and the same time? If it widens any of them, it does not follow.
3. **Do the arithmetic when there are numbers.** Many conclusions are checkable with a quick sum or percentage. Do not estimate when you can calculate.
4. **Look for added causes.** "Because", "led to", "caused", "due to" in a conclusion are warning signs unless the paragraph itself establishes the cause.
5. **Then apply the beyond-reasonable-doubt test.** Would a careful reader accept this as established by the paragraph? If you need to add anything, it does not follow.

## Traps that cost points

- **Changing the unit.** Fewer loans does not mean fewer borrowers. Fewer applications does not mean fewer applicants.
- **Generalising from a sample.** Results from respondents describe respondents, not everyone they were drawn from.
- **Reading correlation as cause.** Two things moving together does not show one drove the other, unless the design or the text establishes it.
- **Predicting the future.** "Will" conclusions rarely follow from data about the past.
- **Skipping the maths.** Several conclusions are traps that look right at a glance and fail on a quick calculation.
- **Holding interpretation to the deduction standard.** If the paragraph clearly establishes something without strict logical necessity, it can still follow here.

## Timing

Interpretation passages often contain numbers. As guidance: allow up to 45 seconds per conclusion where arithmetic is needed and about 30 seconds otherwise. Write the key figures down once per passage so you are not rereading.

## Practice items

These 5 items are original, written for these notes. They are not taken from the Watson-Glaser or any publisher's materials. Assume everything in each paragraph is true.

### Paragraph 1

> Over the past five years, a city's library service has recorded a steady fall in printed books borrowed, from 1.2 million loans a year to 800,000. Over the same period, e-book loans through the library's app rose from 50,000 to 400,000 a year.

**Item 1.** Total loans, printed and e-book together, were lower in the most recent year than five years ago.

**Item 2.** Fewer people use the library service now than five years ago.

**Item 3.** E-book loans grew by more than printed loans fell.

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 1 to 3</summary>

**Item 1: Follows.** Five years ago: 1,200,000 + 50,000 = 1,250,000. Now: 800,000 + 400,000 = 1,200,000. Total loans are lower by 50,000.

**Item 2: Does not follow.** The paragraph counts loans, not people. The same number of people could be borrowing fewer items each, or more people could be borrowing less.

**Item 3: Does not follow.** E-book loans grew by 350,000. Printed loans fell by 400,000. The fall was larger.

</details>

### Paragraph 2

> A law firm surveyed its 200 trainees and junior associates about their development. 45% responded. Of those who responded, 70% said they wanted more feedback at the end of each seat.

**Item 4.** Most of the firm's 200 trainees and junior associates want more feedback at the end of each seat.

**Item 5.** At least 60 respondents said they wanted more feedback at the end of each seat.

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 4 and 5</summary>

**Item 4: Does not follow.** 45% of 200 is 90 respondents, and 70% of 90 is 63 people. 63 out of 200 is not most. The 110 who did not respond could think anything.

**Item 5: Follows.** 70% of 90 is 63, which is at least 60.

</details>

## Score yourself

| Item | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Answer | F | DNF | DNF | DNF | F |

F = follows, DNF = does not follow.

- **5:** good judgment on scope. Keep doing the arithmetic, never estimate.
- **4:** look for a pattern: did you let conclusions widen the group, or skip a calculation?
- **3 or fewer:** before each conclusion, say aloud what the data measured and of whom. Most misses vanish once you do.
