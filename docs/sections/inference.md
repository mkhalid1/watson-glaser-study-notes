# Inference

**In one line:** you read a set of facts and rate how likely a statement is to be true, on a five-point scale from True to False.

Inference is the only Watson-Glaser subtest with five answer options instead of two, which makes it the one where small slips cost the most. It is scored under **Draw Conclusions**, together with deduction and interpretation.

## What it measures

Pearson describes this subtest as rating the probability that an inference is true, based on the information given. In practice it tests whether you can keep three things apart:

- what the facts **prove**
- what the facts **make likely** or **make unlikely**
- what the facts **say nothing about**

Lawyers do this all day. A witness statement proves some things, suggests others, and is silent on the rest. Mixing those up is how weak arguments get made.

## How the questions look

A short passage of facts, usually four to eight sentences, followed by several statements. Each statement is judged on its own against the same passage. You choose one of five labels:

| Label | Choose it when |
|---|---|
| **True (T)** | The statement follows from the facts beyond reasonable doubt. |
| **Probably True (PT)** | The facts make it more likely to be true than false, but do not settle it. |
| **Insufficient Data (ID)** | The facts give no basis for leaning either way. |
| **Probably False (PF)** | The facts make it more likely to be false than true, but do not rule it out. |
| **False (F)** | The statement contradicts the facts, or misreads them. |

Two official rules matter. First, take the passage's facts as true. Second, for the "probably" labels you may use common knowledge that practically everyone shares, such as "people tend to avoid rules that cost them money." You may not use specialist or personal knowledge.

## A method that works

1. **Read the passage once for structure.** Who did what, when, and what is reported as opinion rather than fact ("the manager said", "the survey found").
2. **Read the statement and find its key claim.** Underline, mentally, the words that make it checkable: numbers, "all", "most", "only", time words, cause words.
3. **Ask the True/False question first.** Does the passage prove it, or flatly contradict it? If yes, you are done: T or F.
4. **If not, ask: does anything in the passage point either way?** If nothing does, answer ID.
5. **If something points one way, decide PT or PF.** Name the fact that tips it. If you cannot name one, go back to ID.
6. **Check the neighbour.** Before you click, ask "why not the label next to this one?" True versus Probably True, and Insufficient Data versus Probably True, are where most marks go.

## Traps that cost points

- **Upgrading PT to T.** True needs proof from the passage. If you can think of a realistic way the statement could be false while the facts stay true, it is PT, not T.
- **Using outside knowledge to fill gaps.** If the passage says a company is in Manchester, you do not know its staff live in Manchester. Specialist facts you happen to know do not count.
- **Treating silence as a lean.** When the passage never mentions something, you have no evidence either way, so the answer is ID. Save PF for when a stated fact points against the statement.
- **Reading opinion as fact.** "The director said sales would rise" proves the director said it. It does not prove sales rose.
- **Missing quantity words.** "Most", "all", "some", "only" and "at least" change the answer. A statement about "all" trainees fails if the passage only covers some.
- **Answering the statements as a set.** Several statements about one passage can share the same label, or all differ. Judge each one alone.
- **Confusing F with PF.** False needs a contradiction. If the facts make the statement unlikely but not impossible, it is PF.

## Timing

Inference passages are dense and each one carries several statements, so the reading time is shared. As guidance: spend up to a minute reading the passage carefully the first time, then aim for about 30 seconds per statement. If a statement still feels stuck between two labels after 45 seconds, choose the more cautious label (PT over T, ID over PT) and move on. The cautious label is right more often than not.

## Practice items

These 5 items are original, written for these notes. They are not taken from the Watson-Glaser or any publisher's materials. Each passage has two or three statements. Answer each one, then open the explanations.

### Passage 1

> A regional law firm opened a second office in Leeds in 2022, alongside its original office in Sheffield. Since then, the firm's annual trainee intake has risen from six to ten. Four of the ten trainees in the latest intake are starting their first seat in the Leeds office. The managing partner told staff this year that the Leeds office now brings in more new clients each month than the Sheffield office.

**Item 1.** The Leeds office earns more fee income than the Sheffield office.

**Item 2.** The opening of the Leeds office was a reason for the rise in trainee numbers.

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 1 and 2</summary>

**Item 1: Insufficient Data.** The passage compares *new clients per month*, not fee income. Sheffield has years of existing clients, and new clients may be small or large. Nothing tips the balance. *Why not Probably True:* more new clients is a different measure from more income, and the established office could easily earn more.

**Item 2: Probably True.** The rise in intake began when the Leeds office opened, the rise is four trainees, and four trainees now start in Leeds. That points strongly to the new office driving the increase. *Why not True:* the firm could have grown for other reasons at the same time, such as a new practice area, so the facts do not prove the link. *Why not Insufficient Data:* the timing and the matching numbers are real evidence.

</details>

### Passage 2

> A supermarket chain trialled self-service tills in 40 of its 300 stores for six months. In the trial stores, the average queuing time at peak hours fell from nine minutes to four. Staff in those stores were moved from the tills to restocking shelves, and none were made redundant. The chain has said it will review the results before deciding whether to extend the trial.

**Item 3.** The chain has already decided to install self-service tills in all of its stores.

**Item 4.** During the trial, customers in the trial stores spent less time queuing at peak hours than they did before it.

**Item 5.** The number of staff employed in the trial stores fell during the trial.

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 3 to 5</summary>

**Item 3: False.** The chain says it will review the results *before deciding* whether to extend the trial. A decision to roll out everywhere contradicts that. *Why not Probably False:* this is a direct contradiction, not just an unlikely claim.

**Item 4: True.** Average peak queuing time fell from nine minutes to four in the trial stores. *Why not Probably True:* the passage states it as a measured result.

**Item 5: Probably False.** Staff were moved to other work and none were made redundant, so the trial was not used to cut jobs. *Why not False:* people can leave a job for other reasons, such as moving house or retiring, so the headcount could still have dipped. The facts make a fall unlikely, not impossible.

</details>

## Score yourself

| Item | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Answer | ID | PT | F | T | PF |

- **5:** your inference instincts are sound. Spend your time on the two-option sections.
- **4:** look at which pairs you confused. Most people lose marks on T versus PT or ID versus PT. Reread the method, step 6.
- **3 or fewer:** go back to the five-label table and say the rule for each label out loud before every answer, until it becomes automatic.
