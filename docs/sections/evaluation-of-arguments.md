# Evaluation of arguments

**In one line:** you are given a yes-or-no question and an argument for one side, and you decide whether the argument is strong or weak.

This subtest has its own score area, **Evaluate Arguments**, and Pearson's efficacy report puts 12 of the 40 items in it. It is also where personal opinions do the most damage, because many questions are on charged political, economic and social topics.

## What it measures

Pearson describes this subtest as evaluating the strength and relevance of arguments with respect to a particular question or issue. You are not asked whether you agree. You are asked whether the argument, taken as true, would carry real weight in deciding the question.

This is what a good trainee does when summarising both sides of a dispute for a partner: give each side its best points fairly, and drop the points that sound good but do not bear on the issue.

## How the questions look

A question, usually starting with "Should", followed by several arguments. Each argument starts with "Yes" or "No" and gives a reason. For each one:

| Answer | Choose it when |
|---|---|
| **Strong** | The argument is both important and directly related to the question. |
| **Weak** | The argument is not directly related to the question, or is of minor importance, or deals only with a trivial side of it. |

Two official rules:

- **Treat every argument as true.** Even if you think the claim is false, assume it is correct and judge its weight.
- **"Should" means general welfare.** In the UK edition, a "Should..." question asks whether the action would promote the general welfare of people in the United Kingdom.

## A method that works

1. **Restate the question precisely.** "Should *all* trainees do a client secondment?" is a different question from "Are secondments useful?"
2. **Accept the argument as true.** Say to yourself: "Suppose that is correct."
3. **Test relevance.** Does the argument speak to this exact question, or to a nearby one? If nearby, it is weak.
4. **Test importance.** If true, would it actually change a sensible person's view of the question? Minor inconveniences, cosmetic points and one-off cases are weak.
5. **Ignore your own view.** An argument for the side you disagree with can be strong. An argument for your side can be weak.

## Traps that cost points

- **Judging truth instead of weight.** The test says the argument is true. Arguing with its facts wastes time and leads you to call strong arguments weak.
- **Letting opinion decide.** On charged topics, people mark the side they dislike as weak. Check your answer would be the same if you held the opposite view.
- **Rewarding emotional language.** Words like "dangerous" or "unfair" do not make an argument strong. The content has to bear on the question.
- **Missing scope words.** If the question says "all", an argument showing the rule cannot work in some cases is often strong, because a universal rule has to work everywhere.
- **Calling every serious point strong.** A point can be important in general and still not be directly related to this question.
- **Treating "some people dislike it" as strong.** Mild preferences or inconveniences for some people are usually minor.
- **Assuming a counter-argument makes it weak.** Most strong arguments have replies. That does not reduce their strength.

## Timing

Arguments are short, so this is a section where you can bank time. As guidance: about 25 to 30 seconds per argument. Read the question carefully once; you will use it for every argument that follows.

## Practice items

These 5 items are original, written for these notes. They are not taken from the Watson-Glaser or any publisher's materials. Treat each argument as true.

### Question 1

> Should UK law firms require every trainee to spend one seat on secondment to a client?

**Item 1.** Yes. Trainees who work inside a client's business understand the client's commercial pressures better, which improves the advice they give later in their careers.

**Item 2.** No. Some trainees would have a longer commute for six months.

**Item 3.** No. Many clients do not have the space or the staff to host a trainee, so a rule requiring a secondment for every trainee could not be met at every firm.

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 1 to 3</summary>

**Item 1: Strong.** Better advice from future lawyers is an important benefit, and it follows directly from the secondment experience the question is about.

**Item 2: Weak.** A longer journey for some trainees for a few months is a minor drawback compared with the training value at stake.

**Item 3: Strong.** The question asks about requiring it for *every* trainee. If many clients cannot host trainees, the rule cannot work as written. That goes to the heart of the proposal.

</details>

### Question 2

> Should the voting age in UK general elections be lowered to 16?

**Item 4.** No. Most 16-year-olds have never seen a ballot paper.

**Item 5.** No. In other important areas, such as buying alcohol or serving on a jury, the law does not treat 16-year-olds as adults, and voting should come with the same adult status.

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 4 and 5</summary>

**Item 4: Weak.** Not having seen a ballot paper is trivial. Every new voter of any age sees one for the first time, and it is easily explained.

**Item 5: Strong.** Whether 16-year-olds should be treated as adults is the core of the question, and consistency with how the law treats them elsewhere bears directly on it. You may have a reply, for example that ages differ for good reasons, but a reply does not make the argument weak.

</details>

## Score yourself

| Item | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Answer | S | W | S | W | S |

S = strong, W = weak.

- **5:** you separate weight from opinion well.
- **4:** check whether your misses cluster on one side of a question. If so, your own view may be leaking in.
- **3 or fewer:** go through the method slowly with the sentence "Suppose that is correct" before every argument.
