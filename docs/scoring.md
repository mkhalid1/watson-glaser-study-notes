# How Watson-Glaser scoring works

Short version: you get one mark per correct answer, your raw score out of 40 is compared with a group of people like you, and the result is reported as a percentile. There is no universal pass mark. Each employer decides what score it needs and which comparison group to use.

## Raw score

Every item is either right or wrong. Your raw score is the number you got right, from 0 to 40. Pearson's efficacy report says raw scores can be used to rank candidates, but little else can be read from them on their own.

There is no published penalty for a wrong answer. So never leave an item blank: a guess can only help you.

## Percentile and norm groups

A raw score only means something next to other people's scores. Pearson compares your raw score with a **norm group**: a large sample of people who took the same test, such as graduates or managers. Your **percentile** tells you the share of that group you scored the same as or higher than.

Pearson gives this example in its efficacy report: a raw score of 35 out of 40, compared with the Manager norm, is a percentile rank of 86. That candidate scored equal to or higher than about 86% of managers.

Two things follow from this:

1. **The same raw score can give different percentiles** depending on the norm group the employer picks. A score that looks strong against a general population norm can look ordinary against a group of high-achieving graduates.
2. **Employers can use their own norms.** Pearson says a wide range of norm groups is available, and bespoke company norms can be mapped to the test.

## What your report shows

Pearson's Profile Report gives:

- an **overall percentile**
- a percentile for each of the three areas: **Recognise Assumptions, Evaluate Arguments, Draw Conclusions**
- in a technical section: the number correct, a T-score, a stanine score and a sten score

Pearson also offers a Development Report and an Interview Report to employers. Whether you see any report depends on the employer.

| Score type | What it is |
|---|---|
| Raw score | Number correct, 0 to 40 |
| Percentile | Share of the norm group you matched or beat |
| T-score | A standard score with a mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10 |
| Stanine | A nine-point standard scale, 1 to 9, with 5 in the middle |
| Sten | A ten-point standard scale, 1 to 10, with 5.5 in the middle |

## What counts as a good score

Nobody outside a firm can tell you its cut-off, and firms rarely publish one. You will see numbers like "75%" or "the 80th percentile" quoted online. Treat those as guesswork: Pearson does not publish a pass mark, and the number that matters is the one your employer sets against the norm it chose.

What you can control:

- **Aim to clear every section, not just the total.** Your report shows three area scores. A strong total with a weak Evaluate Arguments score can still stand out to a reviewer.
- **Aim high on practice tests.** If you can score about 32 or more out of 40 on timed, well-made practice, under time pressure, you have a margin for nerves and for harder items on the day. This is our study guidance, not a published standard.
- **Accuracy beats speed, up to a point.** Unanswered items score nothing, but rushed items are where the traps work. The [timing guide](timing.md) shows how to balance the two.

## Why your score can vary between attempts

Because each test is built from an item bank, you will not see the same questions twice. Pearson says item difficulty is allowed for in the scoring, but small swings between attempts are normal. That is one more reason to train the method rather than memorise answers.

Some firms keep a passed score for a period instead of asking you to resit. Clifford Chance, for example, [says it saves a passed Watson Glaser score for two recruitment cycles](https://jobs.cliffordchance.com/faq-london). Check each firm's own rules before you sit the test for it.
